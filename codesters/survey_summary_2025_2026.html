01Who answered, and who didn't

The raw export held 131 rows, but 7 of them are not distinct students. After removing duplicate submissions and one test entry, 124 valid responses remain against 170 enrolled students — a 73% response rate. Of those, 119 can be matched to a known group; 5 cannot. All figures in this report use the cleaned set.

Narva had three impossible rows. Two were removed by the cleaning — the test entry and one duplicate from a Ühisgümnaasium student who filled the form a third time and picked Narva. The 12th-grade group now matches exactly: 3 enrolled, 3 answered. One row remains, an 11th grader writing in Estonian who praises Nikita by name. Since Nikita also mentors both Ühisgümnaasium groups, this is most likely a Ühisgümnaasium student who chose the wrong school.

Tehnikagümnaasium is not a duplication problem. All ten responses carry distinct respondent IDs and none are filler. They arrive as two separate sittings: six on 18 March, one of which names Liuda as their mentor — that is TG24, and six matches the roster exactly — then four more on 6 May, between 11:24 and 11:35, which the roster cannot hold. Those four are now counted separately rather than against TG24, so no group exceeds 100%.

Why the four can't be placed

Splitting the two sittings apart makes TG24 reconcile exactly, but it doesn't say where the 6 May four belong. I compared them against the March six on every dimension that could distinguish a group — mentor rating, platform rating, happiness, grading clarity, all four mentor statements, how they perceive e-learning, career choice, social media awareness. The two batches are indistinguishable. Nothing in the responses identifies a school, a mentor or a class.

Timing gives a partial clue. Early May was when late responses came in generally: single stragglers appear from Kadrioru 11th, Ühisgümnaasium 11th and Tartu 11th between 4 and 9 May. But those are individuals filling the form on their own. Four submissions inside eleven minutes is a group doing it together in a lesson.

Every 11th-grade group still had missing students by then — Ühisgümnaasium was short seven, Kadrioru and Tartu five each, Mustamäe three — so capacity exists almost everywhere and none of it points anywhere in particular. Three explanations remain open: a group missing from the registry, four students from another school who picked the wrong option, or four TG students who enrolled after the roster was last updated. If the mentor teaching around 11:30 on 6 May can recall who was in the room, that settles it in one question.

UG24 is the group to look at. 45% happy — the lowest of any group with more than six responses — with five neutral students and the only unhappy student outside Kadrioru. It is also the group that lost the most respondents to duplicate submissions, and at 61% coverage we are missing seven of its eighteen students.

Mentor quality does not explain these differences. JPG24 rates its mentor 3.85, the lowest in the program, yet 77% of that group is happy. UG24 rates its mentor 4.73 and only 45% are happy. KSG23 gives a perfect 5.00 and sits at 75%.

Whatever separates a happy group from a lukewarm one, it isn't the person teaching them. Timetable position, class culture, and how the group was formed are better places to look.

One number holds almost everywhere: "positive or neutral" is 100% in eleven of the thirteen groups. Only two students in the whole program said they were unhappy, one in KSG23 and one in UG24. 04The central tension: "it still feels like homework"

76 of 124 students say the e-learning lessons still feel like another piece of homework rather than a normal lesson they do at home. This single split predicts almost everything else in the survey.

Among students who say…Neutral or unhappyCan't complete on time
"It still feels like another homework" (n=76)38%33%
"It's a normal lesson I do at home" (n=48)8%10%

27 of the 31 neutral students sit in the "homework" camp, and 25 of the 30 students who miss deadlines do too. The problem isn't the blended model — 88% want to keep the current one live lesson plus two e-learning sessions. It's that the e-learning half isn't landing as lesson time. It reads as extra work stacked on top of school.

Two things in the free text point at the cause. First, load distribution: several students describe quiet weeks followed by a sudden large assignment with no warning. Second, boundaries: some report doing codesters work on Sunday mornings or across the weekend, which makes it feel like it lives outside the school day rather than inside it.

"Some weeks there's nothing to do, and then suddenly you have to do basically a year's worth of things for one assignment. It should be divided better, or make it clear that a bigger assignment is coming so we could start earlier."11th grade

Three things stand out at the bottom.

On-time completion is the weakest item in the survey — 30 students actively disagree, more than any other statement. This is the operational problem to solve.

"Too complex" is emphatically rejected — only 8 students agree. Several students say the opposite in free text: too easy, too slow, stretched over three years when they could ship in a month. The ceiling may be too low for the strongest students.

Time management

64 students break tasks into smaller parts, 52 set specific goals, 49 seek help from a mentor — but 23 use no strategy at all, and only 17 have tried timer techniques. 65 students say they would like to be taught time-management strategies. Given that on-time completion is the weakest item in the survey, that's a standing offer worth taking up.

07Where should the program go?

There is no mandate here. The cohort splits three ways, and the two leading options pull in opposite directions.

University-style · 54
School-style · 46
Tech company · 24

University-style: flexible schedule, one large assignment graded at the end. School-style: more guidance, small clear assignments each graded. Tech company: judged on the final result.

The split maps onto the tension in section 04. Students who want fewer, bigger, later deadlines are asking for autonomy; students who want small graded chunks are asking for structure so they don't fall behind. Both groups are describing the same underlying problem — the current rhythm is unpredictable — and proposing opposite fixes. Whichever direction is chosen, making the calendar visible further ahead serves both.

09Small fixes with outsized returns

The "most lame thing" and "one thing you'd change" questions produced an unusually concrete list. Most of these are platform bugs and workflow annoyances, not curriculum disagreements — which makes them cheap to fix and highly visible when fixed.

  • Dark mode renders some text invisible — named independently by several students, specifically in quizzes. Most-repeated single complaint.
  • Submitting scrolls you back to the top of the page — "I click 'add submission' and I need to scroll the page again. Please fix it, I beg you."
  • Can't open codesters in a second tab without logging out and back in — raised three times, along with a wish to simply stay logged in.
  • Back button returns you to the courses page instead of the previous view — "getting annoying when I am especially tired."
  • Too many third-party tools to track — "I keep forgetting the names and what I had to do in each one." A single index of tools and current tasks was explicitly requested.
  • Video / recorded presentation assignments — disliked by six students; Loom and "recording homework" named directly. Worth checking whether the learning goal needs the camera.
  • No deadline notifications — two students asked for homework alerts in Stuudium or eKool, which is where they already look.
  • Figma frustration — seven mentions, mostly about time cost and getting lost rather than the tool itself.
  • Nobody knows the social media exists — 99 of 124 answered "Wait, you have a social media??" Instagram is the only channel with meaningful reach, at 15 followers in this cohort.

Worth noting the contrast: his group's satisfaction with him is total, yet only 60% say they're happy they joined and 80% still experience e-learning as homework. That gap is a useful reminder that mentor quality and program experience are separable.

Two patterns across the whole set.

First, Discord is where mentoring breaks down. It's the lowest-scoring mentor statement for José, Liuda and Pärli — three mentors covering 82 of the 124 students. Between-lesson availability is the program's weakest link, not classroom teaching.

Second, the mentor score doesn't track the program score. Sten's students rate him 4.80 and the program 60% happy; Zhenyu's rate him 3.85 and the program 77% happy. Whatever makes students happy with codesters.club is largely independent of how they rate the person teaching it.

Notes on the data

  • Two questions are unusable. "How satisfied are you with the program?" and "How likely would you recommend this?" each have only 6 responses out of 124, all submitted between 15 and 23 March. They appear to have been removed or made conditional early in the collection window. No NPS or satisfaction score can be calculated for this year.
  • The sample is self-selected. 124 of 170 enrolled students gave a valid response. Those who disengaged are less likely to have answered, so the 73% happiness figure is probably a ceiling rather than a midpoint — most sharply in the 12th grade, where only 55% answered.
  • Enrolment figures come from the group registry: 42 students in 10th grade, 72 in 11th, 56 in 12th. Two groups do not reconcile with the survey (see section 01).
  • Percentages are of valid responses per question, not of all 124, and are rounded — some rows total 99% or 101%.
  • Duplicate detection used the respondent ID assigned by the form, not names, so two different students sharing a device would not be caught. A few surviving submissions are terse rather than filler ("homework", "class") and were kept.
  • Quotes are reproduced as written, with typos preserved except where a word was unreadable.
